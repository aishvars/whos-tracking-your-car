How Is The Map Built?

This site is a student advocacy project for a graduate course on law, ethics and emerging technology policy at Carnegie Mellon University. It argues for one policy position, set out on the Home page, and says so plainly.

  • Cameras. Locations come from OpenStreetMap, where volunteers tag each camera surveillance:type=ALPR. We query every such point inside the box with corners 40.19, -80.52 to 40.67, -79.69 (south, west to north, east). The box covers Allegheny County and parts of the counties around it. The latest pull was on the latest data date and found – cameras.
  • Vendors. Each camera’s vendor is its OpenStreetMap manufacturer tag. We merge different spellings of the same company (for example “PlateSmart/CyclopsTchnlgs” and “CyclopsTechnologies” become “PlateSmart / Cyclops”), and cameras with no manufacturer tag are shown as “Unknown.”
  • Sensitive places. Churches and other places of worship, clinics, hospitals, doctors’ offices and schools also come from OpenStreetMap. A camera is flagged when one is within 100 metres.
  • Address search, trips and the basemap. Addresses are looked up with Nominatim, driving routes come from the public OSRM server, and the map tiles are OpenStreetMap’s own.
  • Your privacy. This site does not collect or store anything you type, and it has no accounts or analytics. When you search an address or plan a trip, your browser sends the place names directly to Nominatim and OSRM, which handle them under their own policies.

What Can’t The Map Tell You?

  • It is incomplete. Cameras appear only if a volunteer has mapped them. Finding Flock, which counts cameras from the same OpenStreetMap volunteer data, says “an undercount is always more likely than an overcount.”22
  • It is not a county count. Our box is larger than Allegheny County. Published counts for the county differ: 509 according to TribLive, citing Finding Flock in September 2026,22 and 827 according to The Conversation in November 2026.23 We have not reconciled them, so the Home page states only that the county ranks first in Pennsylvania.
  • It is a snapshot. The data is a dated pull, shown on the Home page and the map. New cameras are mapped all the time.
  • Many vendors are unknown. Cameras without a manufacturer tag are labelled “Unknown.” Vendor shares on the Home page describe mapped cameras only.
  • A camera on the map is not proof of practice. The map does not show who runs a camera, how long its data is kept, who can search it or who it is shared with. For that, ask the agency.
  • Nearby is not targeted. A camera within 100 metres of a church, clinic or school does not mean it was placed there for that reason, and only places mapped in OpenStreetMap are counted.
  • Trips are illustrations. A route is computed by the public OSRM server, and a “sighting” is any mapped camera within 30 metres of it, which can include cameras on cross streets or facing the opposite direction. It shows how a trip could be recorded, not that it was.

Credits

  • OpenStreetMap. Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors, available under the Open Database License (ODbL). OpenStreetMap says you may copy, distribute and adapt its data as long as you credit OpenStreetMap and its contributors.28
  • DeFlock. An open-source project that maps license plate readers and, like this site, builds on OpenStreetMap data.29 Its map at maps.deflock.org inspired this one. DeFlock is an advocacy project, and we use it as a pointer, not as a source of facts.
  • Software. Maps are drawn with Leaflet. Addresses are looked up with Nominatim and routes come from OSRM.

Legal Background

This is information, not legal advice.

  • The Fourth Amendment and Carpenter. In Carpenter v. United States (2018), the Supreme Court held that accessing seven days of a person’s cell-site location records is a Fourth Amendment search, because people keep a legitimate expectation of privacy in the record of their physical movements.27 The Court called its decision narrow and said it did not call into question “conventional surveillance techniques and tools, such as security cameras.”27 So Carpenter is not a ruling on license plate readers. It is the closest Supreme Court precedent on location history, and our argument builds on its reasoning.
  • Federal bill. The “Ban Flock Act,” H.R. 10691, was introduced in the House on October 1, 2026 and referred to committees. It has not become law.25
  • Pennsylvania bills. On the General Assembly’s own bill page, House Bill 2771 shows a House vote of 190 to 13 on October 19, 2026, and a last action of referral to a Senate committee on October 26, 2026.26 Check that page for anything later.
  • A patchwork of states. According to Finding Flock, 19 states have laws specifically about ALPRs, and Pennsylvania is not one of them.22
  • Getting records. Pennsylvania’s Right-to-Know Law is how residents ask agencies for their ALPR policies and contracts. The Take action page explains the steps, with official sources.

Figures We Checked

Before publishing, we checked each headline figure against the source it came from. This is what we found, including the caveats.

  • “More than 5,000 agencies use Flock.” This appears on Flock’s own customer page. It is a company claim, and we found no independent count.21
  • “More than 50 communities dropped or paused Flock in 2026.” Stateline reported this on August 14, 2026, citing the DeFlock tracker. It is one tracker’s count on one date, so we date it and attribute it.24
  • “Allegheny County has the most ALPRs in Pennsylvania.” Two news reports say so, and both rest on Finding Flock’s crowdsourced data.22, 23 They give different counts, so we state the ranking and no number.
  • “Congress has seen the Ban Flock Act.” Confirmed: it is H.R. 10691, introduced October 1, 2026. It is a bill, not law.25
  • “Pennsylvania has no comprehensive ALPR law.” Still true as far as we could find, but it needs care: the state House has passed House Bill 2771, which awaits the Senate.26 We checked on November 23, 2026 and recommend checking again before presenting.
  • “Flock now defaults to 7-day retention.” Confirmed as Flock’s own announcement, published August 13, 2026 (the page shows an update on November 11). It says existing customers keep their current periods and that Evidence Mode, for preserving data in active investigations, “will roll out in the coming weeks.”30 It is a company claim about a company setting. Flock’s pages disagree with each other: one says data is deleted after 7 days by default,31 and another, updated October 21, still describes a 30-day default.32 We cannot see what any Pittsburgh-area agency actually uses, which is why our Right-to-Know request asks for it.
  • “Over 90% of searches without a full plate happen within a week.” This is Flock’s own analysis, and we could not check it. It leaves out searches by full plate.30
  • “More than 1,500 agencies have a public transparency portal.” Also Flock’s figure.30 Set against its own claim of more than 5,000 agencies,21 that is no more than about 30%. Flock itself asks agencies without a portal to turn one on.
  • “Fewer than 9 human-reported errors per million alerts.” Flock’s figure.30 It counts errors that people report, and it is not measured the same way as the Roseville police analysis on the How It Works page, so we do not compare the two.
  • “Customers own their data, and sharing is off by default.” Flock’s plain-English guide says so and quotes its contract. The same contract text gives Flock a “limited, non-exclusive, royalty-free, irrevocable, perpetual, worldwide license” to use customer data to provide its services and to support and improve them.33 We do not claim any misuse. It is one reason our request asks each agency for its contract.
  • “Courts have found that Flock is not mass surveillance.” Flock’s page title refers to 30 courts.32 We have not verified those cases, and Carpenter is narrow, so this site neither relies on the claim nor disputes it.
  • Where a source has a point of view. The EFF and DeFlock are advocacy groups, and Flock is a company. Wherever we use them, the text says who said it, and each source in the list below is labelled by kind.
