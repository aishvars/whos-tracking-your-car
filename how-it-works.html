How your data moves

Follow one license plate from the moment a camera sees it. Five steps, the risks at each one, and where our four asks apply.

Step 1 of 5

Step 1 of 5

A camera photographs every plate that passes

Automated license plate readers (ALPRs) are cameras mounted on poles, overpasses and patrol cars. They automatically capture every license plate that comes into view, along with the location, date and time.1 Software reads the plate's characters from the image, and the photograph of the vehicle is kept too.1, 5

One major vendor, Flock Safety, says its cameras collect plate images, vehicle characteristics, date and time stamps and a camera location, and that they collect no biometrics and use no facial recognition.3

Risk: misreads

The software can read a character wrong, such as a 3 for a 7 or an H for an M.6 In Roseville, California, police found that 1,011 of 1,427 alerts for stolen or felony-linked vehicles in 2023 and 2024 (71%) involved a misread. The police say none led to a stop or arrest, and Flock says that figure covers only those alerts, not all plate reads.5

What our proposal keeps Cameras and real-time alerts for stolen and wanted cars stay. The four asks change what happens to the data afterwards.

Step 2 of 5

The scan is uploaded, stored and indexed

Each read is uploaded to a central server.1 Flock says its data is encrypted on the device, in transit and when stored, and a 2022 Flock fact sheet describes storing it in the cloud.3, 4

How long it stays is a local choice. Flock's current trust page says each customer sets its own retention period and that Flock recommends 7 days;3 its 2022 fact sheet described a 30-day default.4

Risk: long retention

Kept over time, scans stop being isolated sightings. They can show every time a plate passed a location, which lets someone infer where the driver likely lives or works.1

Step 3 of 5

Officers search it, and hot lists trigger alerts

Agencies load hot lists of the plates they are looking for, such as stolen vehicles or vehicles tied to warrants. When a camera reads a plate on the list, the system alerts the officer or agency.1 Hot lists can also include low-level misdemeanors and traffic offenses.2

Officers can also search the stored scans. Flock says every search is tied to a user and logged, and that each search needs a public-safety reason that is recorded with it.3

Risk: a wrong match

In Aurora, Colorado, in August 2020, an ALPR flagged Brittney Gilliam's SUV as a stolen vehicle. The stolen vehicle was a motorcycle with Montana plates; hers had Colorado plates. Police detained her family at gunpoint and handcuffed them, including the children, and the city later settled her lawsuit for $1.9 million.6

Risk: searches with little oversight

EFF reported that a Johnson County official ran a search with the note "had an abortion, search for female" that covered 6,809 networks and 83,345 cameras.7 The Texas county's sheriff and Flock said the woman was being checked on because her family feared for her safety.8 EFF also reports that agencies sometimes used vague wording in the reason field for searches tied to protests.7

Step 4 of 5

It can be shared beyond the agency that bought it

Customers choose their sharing settings, and Flock says nothing is shared unless they turn sharing on.3 But sharing can reach far: EFF reports that a single search covered 6,809 networks and 83,345 cameras across nearly the entire country.7

Citing audit data analyzed by 404 Media, EFF reports more than 4,000 nationwide and statewide lookups by local and state police that were done at the request of federal agencies, as informal favors, or with a possible immigration focus, although ICE had no formal agreement with Flock.9 In Illinois, the Secretary of State opened an audit after EFF research reported that Flock allowed U.S. Customs and Border Protection to access Illinois data in violation of state privacy laws.7

Risk: over-sharing

EFF argues that informal sharing lets federal agencies reach the network through local partnerships, without the transparency and legal limits that would apply to a direct contract.9

Step 5 of 5

It adds up to a travel history, built without suspicion

One scan seems harmless. In aggregate, ALPR data can reveal a vehicle's historical travel and regular patterns, and with algorithms it can predict where a driver may be in the future. It also reveals everyone who visited a particular location. The data is gathered indiscriminately, on millions of ordinary people.1

Try it on the map: follow a trip from one neighborhood to another and see how many cameras record it.
